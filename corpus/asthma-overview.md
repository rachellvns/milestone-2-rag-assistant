## Phenotypes

Asthma is increasingly understood as a heterogeneous condition rather than a single disease. Allergic asthma, often beginning in childhood, is associated with elevated IgE and sensitization to common allergens such as dust mites, pollen, or pet dander. Non-allergic asthma, more common in adult-onset cases, may be triggered by respiratory infections, irritant exposure, or hormonal factors and is less clearly linked to atopy. Eosinophilic asthma, which may occur in either allergic or non-allergic patients, is characterized by elevated eosinophil counts in blood or sputum and often responds well to biologic therapies targeting the eosinophilic pathway. Obesity-related asthma represents another recognized phenotype, often presenting with fewer classic allergic features and a distinct inflammatory profile.

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis relies on a combination of characteristic symptoms and objective demonstration of variable airflow limitation. Spirometry showing an obstructive pattern with significant bronchodilator reversibility, typically defined as an increase in FEV1 of at least 12 percent and 200 mL after bronchodilator administration, supports the diagnosis. When spirometry is normal but suspicion remains high, bronchial provocation testing with methacholine or exercise challenge can help demonstrate airway hyperresponsiveness. Peak flow variability monitored over one to two weeks can also support diagnosis, particularly in primary care settings without ready access to specialist testing.

## Triggers

Common triggers include respiratory viral infections, allergen exposure, cold air, exercise, strong odors or irritants, and certain medications such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or beta-blockers in susceptible individuals. Occupational asthma, triggered by exposure to specific substances in the workplace such as isocyanates, flour dust, or laboratory animal proteins, is an important and often underrecognized cause that warrants careful occupational history taking, since removal from exposure can be central to management.

## Severity Classification

Asthma severity is typically classified based on symptom frequency, nighttime awakenings, use of rescue medication, and degree of activity limitation prior to treatment, ranging from intermittent to mild, moderate, and severe persistent disease. However, once treatment has begun, control — meaning how well symptoms are managed on current therapy — becomes the more clinically relevant measure, since a patient on appropriate treatment for severe disease may nonetheless demonstrate good day-to-day control.

## Comorbidities

Adults with asthma frequently have coexisting conditions that can worsen control if left unaddressed, including allergic rhinitis, chronic rhinosinusitis, gastroesophageal reflux disease, obesity, and obstructive sleep apnea. Anxiety and depression are also more prevalent among patients with asthma and can both mimic and exacerbate perceived symptom burden, making a holistic assessment important when symptoms seem disproportionate to objective findings.
